ISS — LAB 1: Odometry on the AlphaBot robot 
Implementation guide. 
Robot: Waveshare AlphaBot with an Arduino UNO. Slotted wheel encoders. 
Acceptance criterion 
One criterion, taken straight from the assignment: 
1. The operator drives the robot manually from a marked point A to a point B. 2. The robot is 
carried back to point A. A sequence of commands is sent to it over    the serial interface, and the 
robot drives from A to B autonomously. 
Everything else in this guide serves that single drive. The numeric values given in stages 2–4 are 
diagnostic aids, not pass/fail thresholds — they tell you whether a given building block works before 
you build the next one on top of it.

Ground rules 
Manufacturer's libraries are not allowed 
You may not use the `AlphaBot` library or the `E01`–`E12` examples supplied by Waveshare — 
neither through #include nor by copying fragments of them. The motor layer, encoder handling, 
odometry, the controllers and the protocol are all to be written from scratch. 
There are two reasons. First, the assignment is about understanding and designing those layers, not 
about calling them. Second, the manufacturer's library contains bugs that matter here — speed is 
stored as a char (range −128…127), so setting a value above 127 overflows the variable, and the PID 
example feeds the right motor with the output of the left wheel's controller. Building on that means 
debugging somebody else's mistakes instead of your own controller. 
Allowed: the standard Arduino libraries (Arduino.h, Serial, avr/interrupt.h). 
Your own work 
The guide describes what each element has to do and how to check that it works. It contains no 
ready-made implementation. The structure of the program, the choice of data types, the organisation 
of the control loop and the protocol format are part of the assignment. 

Every stage ends with a checklist. Do not move on to the next stage until the current one is ticked off — 
stages 3–6 rely on numbers measured in stages 1, 2 and 4.

Hardware 
The AlphaBot board carries a lot of peripherals. This assignment uses three blocks: the H-bridge that 
drives the motors, the wheel speed sensors and the serial interface. The remaining blocks stay unused 
and their jumpers have to be removed. 

 
AlphaBot platform 
Manufacturer's catalogue photo. Visible: the main board with the holder for two 18650 cells, the driven 
wheels with their motors, the caster underneath, the power switch and the UART switch. The module 
at the front of the chassis and some of the connectors belong to peripherals this assignment does not 
use. 
The platform is controlled by the Arduino UNO alone. There is no single-board computer on the robot, 
and every timing decision — encoder interrupts, the control loop period, serial handling — belongs to 
the microcontroller. That is a real design constraint: the ATmega328P runs at 16 MHz and has 2 kB of 
RAM, two external interrupts and one hardware UART. Every solution has to fit inside that. 
The drive path 
The path from the microcontroller to the wheel has four links: 
1. microcontroller outputs — six signals, three per wheel, 
2. field A jumpers — they select whether the signals come from the Arduino, 
3. L298P H-bridge — it switches the supply voltage onto the windings, 
4. motor with gearbox and the wheel with its encoder disc.

The H-bridge — what you need to know about it 
A single bridge channel is four switches in an H arrangement with the motor winding connected 
between them. Two direction inputs drive the two halves independently, and the enable input opens all 
the switches of the channel.

Schematic of the drive block 
 
H-bridge schematic on the AlphaBot board 
The manufacturer's schematic. It shows three things the state table does not: 
– freewheeling diodes D1–D8 across the outputs. A motor winding is an inductance — when the 
switches change state the current cannot disappear instantly and needs a return path. Under PWM 
control these diodes conduct in every modulation period; 
– the `SENSEA` and `SENSEB` inputs tied to ground. The L298 lets you insert sense resistors 
there and measure the motor current; this board does not use that option, so motor current is not 
available as a feedback signal; 
– decoupling capacitors at the supply pins. 
Note how the supplies are separated: VIN feeds the power stage and the outputs, while the logic of the 
chip runs from the 5 V rail. That is why the USB interface alone is not enough to run the drive. 
Why telling coasting and braking apart matters: a spinning motor is a generator. With the winding open 
its kinetic energy is dissipated only in friction; with the winding shorted it is also dissipated in the 
winding resistance. For the position controller of stage 3 the difference amounts to a few centimetres 
of travel after the stop command. 
Speed sensors 
An encoder disc with radial slots sits on the extension of the gearbox output shaft, on the opposite side 
from the wheel. A WYC-H206 slotted opto-interrupter straddles the rim of the disc: an infrared emitter 
on one side, a receiver on the other. The disc interrupts the beam, and the sensor output passes 
through a conditioning stage that produces a clean square wave. 

 
Speed sensor module 
 
Schematic of the speed sensor module 
The manufacturer's photo and schematic. The circuit is simple: the opto-interrupter, two resistors (one 
limiting the emitter current, one pulling up the receiver output) and an integrated circuit that conditions 
the DOUT output signal. 
This is a good moment to compare two sources. The manufacturer's manual describes that stage as a 
Schmitt trigger, while the schematic shows a specific integrated circuit — read off its part number and 
check in the datasheet what it actually is and whether the prose description agrees. Spotting that kind 
of discrepancy is part of working with documentation; one sentence in the report on what came out of 
it is enough.

Three properties of this sensor shape the whole solution: 
A single channel. The sensor gives one signal. There is no second one shifted by a quarter period, so 
the direction of rotation cannot be read from the signal itself — the counter increases the same way 
whether the robot drives forwards or backwards. Dealing with that is a task for stage 2. 
Measurement behind the gearbox. The disc sits on the same shaft as the wheel, only on the other 
side of the gearbox, so it turns exactly as many times as the wheel does. The gear ratio therefore does 
not enter the distance calculation: one unknown fewer. The price is resolution — it is worse by exactly 
the gear ratio than it would be with the disc on the motor shaft ahead of the gearbox. 
Check this yourself on the robot: turn the wheel through one full revolution and count the revolutions of 
the disc. The 1:1 assumption is the foundation of the whole odometry, so it should not remain an 
assumption. 
Resolution is the bottleneck of this assignment. The wheel travel per count follows from the wheel 
circumference and the number of counts per revolution. Working it out is the first thing to do in stage 2, 
because it sets the limit on turn accuracy in stage 4 and a sensible stopping tolerance in stage 3. 
Both external interrupts of the ATmega328P are taken. INT0 and INT1 serve the encoders; no other 
peripheral in this assignment may use external interrupts. 
Encoder discs 

 
Encoder discs 
Two discs in the kit, one per wheel. The central hole is shaped to match the hub, so the disc turns with 
the wheel without slipping. The slots are radial and evenly spaced around the circumference. 
Two things to watch during assembly, because both corrupt the measurement in a way that is hard to 
diagnose from the program: 
– the disc has to pass inside the slot of the opto-interrupter without rubbing against either wall. 
Rubbing brakes the wheel and wears the disc down over time; 
– the disc has to be mounted without play. A loose disc vibrates when the direction changes and 
produces counts with no distance travelled. 
The complete drive 

 
Drive with the encoder disc 
Photo of the actual setup. The whole path is visible: the wheel on the gearbox output shaft, the yellow 
gearbox housing, the DC motor behind it, and on the far side of the gearbox — the encoder disc on the 
extension of that same shaft, inside the slot of the opto-interrupter screwed to the gearbox housing. 
The motor leads go to the board connector labelled with the channel name. 
What to look at when checking the assembly: 

– the disc passes through the opto-interrupter slot without touching either wall, 
– the opto-interrupter is screwed down firmly — a loose one shifts relative to the disc and starts 
losing pulses when the robot is jolted, 
– the motor leads go to the connector matching the correct side of the robot. Swapping the left and 
right motor leads produces a symptom that is easy to mistake for a software bug: the robot turns 
the wrong way even though driving straight looks correct. 
To be counted before starting stage 2 
Count the slots on the encoder disc. That number is deliberately not given in this guide — it enters the 
formula for the travel per count, so an error in it propagates into the whole odometry. 
How: remove the disc, or mark a reference point on the rim and count the slots while going around the 
disc. Verify the result electrically: turn the wheel by exactly one revolution and compare the number 
of counts with your prediction. Agreement between the two methods confirms that both the disc and 
the interrupt configuration are what you assume them to be. 
Do the count for both wheels — do not assume the discs are identical. 
Serial interface 
The Arduino UNO has one hardware UART, brought out through a USB converter to the connector. The 
same link carries both program upload and the protocol from stage 5, so the port can be held by only 
one tool at a time. 
The UART receive buffer in the Arduino library has a limited capacity. Look its value up in the 
documentation — it is needed in stage 5 to decide the flow control rule. 
Power 
Two 18650 cells in series give 7.4 V. The voltage goes through the on-board converter to the logic and 
directly to the bridge as the motor supply. 
The drive is not powered from USB. With the power switch off or the cells flat, the Arduino runs, 
answers over serial and looks healthy while the robot stands still. That is the first hypothesis for the 
symptom "it does not move". 
Take all calibration measurements at a similar state of charge and note the voltage. A drop from 8.4 V 
to 7.0 V changes the speed at the same PWM duty by more than ten per cent, which invalidates the 
ramps and dead-band thresholds you have chosen.

Stage 1 — Bringing up the drive, straight line and turning 
Goal 
A working open-loop motor layer, and a feel for how the robot behaves without control. 
What to implement 
Motor layer. A function that sets the speed of one wheel, taking a signed value covering the full PWM 
range in both directions. The function sets the direction inputs according to the sign and puts the 
magnitude on the enable input. 
Mind the data type: the range has to cover full PWM in both directions. This is exactly where the 
manufacturer's library has the bug described at the start of the guide. 
Stopping functions. Coasting and dynamic braking separately — for the comparison in the 
measurement below. 
Three motion modes, as combinations of the two wheel speeds: 
Mode 
Description

both wheels the same way

wheels counter-rotating, turn about the centre of the track

The pivot mode will be useful in stage 4 — worth having from the start. 
Direction check 
Put the robot on a stand (wheels in the air) and check each mode separately, in both directions. 
Confirm which way each wheel turns. 
If a wheel turns the wrong way, apply the correction in the code — a constant that inverts the sign for 
that wheel — and write it down. A correction in the code is explicit and gets documented; re-plugging 
the motor connectors does not. 
Measurements 
PWM dead band. For each wheel separately, find the smallest PWM duty at which the wheel turns 
continuously (without stuttering). Take the measurement with the wheels on the floor — under load 
the threshold is higher than on a stand. 
For the later stages adopt a single value: the larger of the two measured, increased by a margin of 
about 20 %. The reason will become clear in stage 3 — the controller subtracts a correction from one 
wheel's PWM, so it needs headroom above the threshold. 
If the two thresholds differ by more than about 25 %, check the mechanics (a rubbing encoder disc, 
play in the gearbox) before going further. 
Run-on distance. For two PWM values — medium and high — measure how far the robot travels after 
the drive is removed. Separately for coasting and for dynamic braking. 
The result of this measurement answers the question of why, in stage 3, the condition "target reached 
→ stop" is not enough on its own.

Stage 2 — Wheel distance and speed from the encoders 
Goal 
A signed distance counter for each wheel, and a calibrated coefficient converting counts to millimetres. 
Background 
Counts per revolution 
Let S be the number of slots in the disc — counted on the robot, as instructed in the hardware chapter. 
The opto-interrupter is blocked and unblocked in turn, so one wheel revolution is S signal periods, and 
each period contains two edges. 
The number of counts per wheel revolution depends on the interrupt configuration: 
N_rev = S (reacting to one edge), N_rev = 2S (reacting to both edges) 
The choice is a design decision. Resolution is the bottleneck of this assignment — it is worth thinking 
through what happens to the accuracy of a small turn (stage 4) under each variant before making the 
decision. 
Converting to distance 
k = (π D)/N_rev 
where D is the wheel diameter and k the travel of the wheel circumference per count. 
The manufacturer does not give the wheel diameter in the documentation. Measure it with callipers, 
but derive the working value by calibration over a distance — and use that one. 
The resolution of the distance measurement, equal to k, comes out at a few millimetres. That means 
the distance measurement is quantised, and no position controller can have a stopping tolerance 
smaller than one or two counts. The consequences for turning are in stage 4. 
The direction problem 
The opto-interrupter has one channel. There is no second, phase-shifted signal, so the direction of 
rotation cannot be read from the sensor output — the counter increases equally when driving 
forwards and backwards. 
The solution has to be designed by you. The questions the design must answer, and which are worth 
discussing in the report: 
– where to get the sign of the distance from, if the sensor does not supply it, 
– what should happen to the counter when the robot is standing still and the disc vibrates, 
– what should happen to the counter while coasting, when the drive is already off but the robot is 
still moving (the run-on measurement from stage 1 shows how much that is). 
The last two points are in conflict: a solution that cuts the counts off as soon as the drive stops loses 
the coasting distance; one that does not cut them off collects counts from vibration. The compromise is 
part of the assignment. 
Interrupt service routine 
The interrupt service routine should be as short as possible — no arithmetic, no printing to serial. All 
conversions belong in the main loop. 

Variables shared between the interrupt and the main loop need two things: the right type qualifier and 
an atomic read in the main loop. The ATmega328P is 8-bit, so reading a variable wider than a byte can 
be interrupted halfway and return a value assembled from two different states. How to protect the read 
is for you to decide; note that the mechanism must not enable interrupts in a situation where they were 
disabled before the call.

Measurements 
Calibration of $k$. Mark out a section on the floor (at least 1 m is recommended), place the robot with 
its wheel axis over the start line, drive and stop exactly on the finish line, read off the counts. Three 
repetitions plus one in reverse.

diameter, and explain the difference. A spread between runs above about 2 % points to a flaw in the 
method, not in the hardware. 
Static PWM → speed characteristic, for each wheel separately, for at least five PWM values across the 
useful range. Two things needed later follow from it: 
– the operating point for cruising — inside the linear part of the characteristic, 

– the wheel asymmetry, that is the speed difference at the same PWM. This is the quantity the 
controller of stage 3 has to compensate; note it down, because the larger it is, the more clearly the 
controller's effect shows. 
If the asymmetry exceeds about 15 % or changes sign across the PWM range, check the mechanics — 
the controller will not fix that.

Stage 3 — Straight-line controller for a given distance 
Goal 
The command "drive the given number of millimetres forwards or backwards", executed in a feedback 
loop closed by the encoders. 
Background 
Two controllers, two different jobs 
The straight-line controller consists of two independent parts acting on the same pair of motors: 
The distance controller answers the question how fast to drive given how far is left to the target, and 
when to stop. It is not a proportional controller — it is a speed profile generator with a stopping 
condition. 
The symmetry controller answers the question how much to differentiate the wheels in order to drive 
straight. This is the one required by the assignment: a proportional controller based on the difference 
of the left and right wheel odometry. 
Quantities 
Let N_L, N_R be the counts since the start of the motion and k the coefficient from stage 2. 
Progress along the path: 
s = (N_L + N_R)/2 k 
Heading error expressed as the difference of the wheel distances: 
e = (N_L - N_R) k 
For a differential drive with track b that difference is tied to the change in orientation: 
Δθ = (s_R - s_L)/b 
So by driving e to zero you hold the orientation from the moment of the start — the robot drives 
straight. 
A note on interpretation: the controller zeroes the orientation, not the lateral deviation. A robot that 
has drifted off and straightened up will carry on straight, but parallel to the intended direction, with the 
offset it has accumulated. That is why it matters that the error should not build up, rather than that it 
should be corrected after the fact. 
Control law 
A correction proportional to the difference of counts, added to one wheel and subtracted from the 
other. Unit of the gain: PWM units per count of difference. 
To be designed: 
– how to get the sign of the correction right when driving backwards, where both counters are 
negative, without branching the code into two cases, 
– what to do when the correction is so large that it would push one wheel's PWM below the dead 
band from stage 1. Consider what physically happens to that wheel then, and whether the 
controller is still improving what it was meant to improve. 
Speed profile 

A ramp for acceleration and deceleration is needed, driven by position rather than time. 
Why it is necessary: 
– at the start — applying PWM as a step makes the wheels slip, and slip means counts with no 
distance travelled, that is an odometry error no controller can detect; 
– at the end — the run-on distance measured in stage 1 shows how far the robot travels after the 
drive is removed. Slowing down before the target reduces that distance. 
Choose the ramp length from the run-on measurement. 
Note the side effect: during the ramp the PWM is close to the dead band, so the headroom for the 
symmetry correction is small or zero. Lengthening the ramp improves the smoothness of the start and 
worsens the heading correction there. That is a genuine trade-off — worth measuring and describing. 
Choosing the gain 
There is no formula. The starting reasoning: decide at what error (in counts) the correction should 
reach some fraction of the cruising PWM, and compute the gain from that. When choosing both 
numbers, take into account the encoder resolution from stage 2 and the headroom above the dead 
band. 
Then tune on the robot. It is worth providing for changing the gain by a command from the terminal, 
without recompiling — tuning by uploading the program every time is incomparably slower.

Stage 4 — Turn controller for a given angle 
Goal 
The command "turn by a given angle to the left or to the right", with the accuracy required by the 
assignment (at least 45°), and measured accuracy for 15, 30, 45 and 90°. 
Background 
Kinematic model 
For a differential drive with track b: 
Δθ = (s_R - s_L)/b 
From this formula, derive the distance a wheel has to travel to produce a given turn — separately for 
the spin turn and for the pivot turn. The two results differ by a factor of 2. 
Put the derivation in the report; the conclusion about resolution below follows from it. 
Sign convention 
Adopt and record in the protocol description (stage 5) an unambiguous convention: which direction of 
rotation corresponds to a positive angle. Mixing conventions is the most common source of errors 
when assembling the command sequence in stage 6. 
The wheel track is a calibration parameter 
The track measured with a tape is not the same quantity as the one in the formula. The reasons: 
– tyre width — the contact point is not a point; during a turn the tyre twists and the effective contact 
point shifts, 
– lateral slip — in a spin turn the tyres have to slide, 
– the caster — its friction when the direction changes introduces a moment that moves the centre of 
rotation. 
That is why the track has to be calibrated from an angle measurement, not measured. 
Angular resolution — the key limitation of this assignment 
One encoder count corresponds to a distance k along the wheel circumference. From the kinematic 
model, derive the smallest change in orientation the controller is able to notice — separately for the 
spin turn and for the pivot turn. Work those values out for your own robot, substituting k from stage 2 
and the track from the calibration below. 
Then work out how many counts per wheel a 15° turn amounts to, and compare that with the stopping 
tolerance. 
The conclusion from this calculation matters for the whole assignment: the 45° required by the 
assignment is achievable without difficulty, while 15° lies close to the resolution limit of the sensor.

Stage 5 — Communication protocol over the serial interface 
Goal 
The robot is to execute a route given as a single string sent over the serial interface. 
Command format 
The operator sends a sequence of commands separated by spaces. Each command is a command 
letter followed by a number: 
G1.5 L45 R-45 G2 
That means: drive 1.5 m straight, turn 45° left, turn 45° right, drive 2 m straight.

Characters other than a command letter are ignored. Spaces, line endings and anything the robot does 
not recognise simply trigger no action. That way there is no need to check whether the operator 
separated the commands with a space, a comma, or sent them over several lines. 
To be decided: in the example R-45 means a 45° turn to the right, so the direction is carried by the

direction follows from the command letter alone. Describe the rule you adopt in the report.

drive has to be copyable from the terminal.

Why switch rather than a chain of conditionals: 
– the decision is in one place and all the command letters are visible at once; adding another 
command is one more case, not another branch tacked on at the end, 
– the default branch is explicit handling of unrecognised characters, which is exactly what the 
protocol requires — with a chain of conditionals, skipping a character is merely a side effect of no 
condition having matched, 
– lower and upper case can be handled by one case: case 'G': case 'g': — whether the protocol 
should distinguish them is for you to decide. 

Mind the break at the end of each case. Omitting it falls through to the next case, which with this 
protocol means executing a different motion from the one intended — and the compiler does not warn 
about it by default. 
Serial.parseFloat() waits for characters until it has assembled a number or its own timeout expires. 
In the stage 5 test sketch that is acceptable. In stage 6, where a motion takes seconds, you have to 
decide what happens to commands that arrived in the meantime — and whether the whole string 
should be read before the drive starts or during it. 
 

Stage 6 — Autonomous A → B drive 
Goal 
The acceptance scenario from the assignment. Nothing new to write beyond merging stages 1–5 into 
one working program. 
Procedure 
Phase 1 — establishing the route. The robot stands at point A, on the start orientation mark. The 
operator types single commands into the serial monitor — G0.8, L90, G1.2 and so on — watches the 
robot and picks the next one, until it comes to rest at point B. Every command used is written down. 
Phase 2 — autonomous drive. The robot goes back to point A, onto the same mark. The operator 
sends one string made up of the recorded commands, for example: 
G0.8 L90 G1.2 R45 G0.6 
The robot executes the whole route with no further intervention. 
Phase 3 — measurement. The deviation of the wheel axis from point B and the heading deviation. 
Repeat phases 2 and 3 three times, without repeating phase 1, and compute the spread. 
How to record the route in phase 1 
The command sequence has to be the one that will actually be sent in phase 2 — not a log of everything 
the operator typed along the way. Two things to decide: 
– corrections. If after G1.0 it turned out that another G0.3 was needed, the sequence should contain 
G1.3, not both commands. Two consecutive turns the same way add up as well; 
– reversals. If the operator overshot and backed up, the difference goes into the sequence. Not 
every such correction can be merged — a turn performed between two straight sections changes 
the geometry and cannot be folded in. 
Establishing the route this way is slower than driving with a joystick, but it has an advantage: the 
command sequence emerges directly in the form in which it will be sent, with no intermediate 
conversion step.
